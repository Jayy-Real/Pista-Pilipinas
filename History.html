History & culture

A pista is a town fiesta, and in the Philippines almost every one begins with faith, a harvest, or a hard-won recovery. These festivals show how Indigenous traditions, Spanish-era Catholicism and local resilience blend into the countrys loudest, most colorful celebrations.

How each festival began

1521 to 1980: Sinulog

Rooted in the 1521 gift of the Santo Niño image to Cebu’s queen. The sulog dance mimics a river’s flow, and the modern parade began in 1980.

1995: Panagbenga

A Kankanaey word for a season of blossoming, launched to lift Baguio’s spirits after the 1990 earthquake.

1807: Moriones

Recalls Longinus, the Roman centurion converted at the Crucifixion. Locals wear carved masks and armor, a tradition traced to at least 1807.

Spanish era: Pahiyas

Thanks San Isidro Labrador, patron of farmers, with houses decorated in kiping rice wafers, fruit and vegetables.

1970 to 1988: Kadayawan

From dagayaw, a word of Davao’s Indigenous peoples for something valuable and magnificent. It grew from the 1970 Apo Duwaling festival and was renamed in 1988.

1980: MassKara

Born from a sugar-price crisis and a ferry tragedy, when Bacolod answered with smiling masks and dancing.

Festival etiquette

  • Arrive early and book rooms months ahead.
  • Bring water, sunscreen and a rain layer.
  • Ask before photographing masked performers or devotees.
  • Keep valuables close in dense crowds.
